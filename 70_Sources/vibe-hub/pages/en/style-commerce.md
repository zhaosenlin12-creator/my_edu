# DTC E-commerce

You might say

This is a brand's store, so it should have personality, tell a story, and make people want to buy.

**Let product, brand feeling, and buying information work together toward a decision**·DTC, or direct-to-consumer, describes a business model rather than a visual style. This example shows a common branded e-commerce pattern: the product leads visually while price, options, reviews, delivery, and the purchase action remain easy to understand. Lifestyle imagery and editorial typography can create desire, but the page cannot behave like a lookbook alone.

Design Principles

- **Keep the product in the lead**: photography, type, and decoration should help people understand the item and experience
- **Keep buying facts close to the action**: price, option, availability, delivery, and returns should not be hidden
- **Use the brand story to support choice**: material, process, and point of view should explain why the product is worth buying
- **Make reviews specific and credible**: show volume, source, and real use context rather than five stars alone

### When to use it

- **Beauty, fashion, food, and lifestyle brands** with visually distinctive products and stories

- **Brands selling directly to customers**, where the website must express identity and convert

- **Products that benefit from material, process, or usage guidance** to reduce uncertainty
- **Businesses driven by repeat and bundle purchases**, where recommendations and sets belong naturally in the journey

### When NOT to use it

- **Interrupt with offers before showing the product**; repeated popups damage judgment and brand confidence

  **SALE SALE SALE**Five popups before the product appears
- **Use beautiful images without buying facts**, leaving price, options, and delivery hard to find
- **Label every item a must-have bestseller**; without difference, the language cannot help anyone choose
- **Place the mobile purchase action far away**; it should remain easy to reach through longer content
