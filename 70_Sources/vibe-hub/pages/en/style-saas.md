# SaaS Marketing Site

You might say

Make it look like a software company website: a big hero headline, product screenshots, customer logos, and pricing.

**Place the value, product proof, and next action on one clear reading path**·A SaaS marketing site is a common website pattern, not a formal visual movement. It organizes a conversion path around the problem the product solves: a focused hero states the promise, real product views and customer evidence build trust, and a consistent call to action moves the visitor toward a trial or conversation. The visual style can vary, but decoration cannot replace product proof or a clear next step.

Design Principles

- **Make one promise above the fold**: say who the product serves, what it changes, and why the visitor should keep reading
- **Show the real product early**: a credible interface, workflow, or outcome explains more than an abstract glow
- **Place proof along the story**: customers, reviews, numbers, and cases should support the claim beside them
- **Keep one primary action**: choose a trial, demo, or sales conversation as the main direction

### When to use it

- **SaaS, AI tools, and developer products** that must explain capability and encourage signup

  **One promise**Product proofStart free
- **Products with a useful interface to show**, where the real workflow can become the visual lead

  Customer logo (permission required)

  Customer ACustomer BCustomer C
- **Longer sales journeys** that need evidence and scenarios to reduce perceived risk
- **Brands that will keep expanding** and benefit from reusable product, data, and content modules

### When NOT to use it

- **Fill the first screen with feature cards** before the visitor understands what the product is

  **Everything. Everywhere.**12 equal feature cards compete at once
- **Use gradients instead of product proof**, leaving a technology mood but no concrete understanding
- **Present several competing primary actions** for trial, purchase, contact, and download
- **Copy another SaaS company's structure and language**; specificity builds more trust than familiarity
